**TLDR:** Explore Kimi K3’s efficient architecture, stable latent MoE, and Kimi Delta Attention. Learn how Chinese AI models reshape inference costs and semiconductor demand.

Something interesting happened last year. [Deepseek](https://medium.com/@tahirbalarabe2/deepseek-r1-explained-chain-of-thought-reinforcement-learning-and-model-distillation-0eb165d928c9?sharedUserId=tahirbalarabe2) caught up to OpenAI. Then Kimi K3 caught up to Anthropic and OpenAI. Not just on benchmarks. The question people are asking now is whether Chinese models will lead AI innovation.

That’s the obvious question. But it’s not the interesting one.

The interesting question is about what happens beneath the surface. Not the benchmarks. Not the political implications. The architecture. Because when you look at how Kimi K3 actually works, you see something different than what everyone is talking about.

This model is about efficiency. Real efficiency. Not the kind that shows up in a press release. The kind that changes what’s possible.

Let me explain what I mean.

## The Expert Problem

Everyone knows about [mixture of experts](https://medium.com/@tahirbalarabe2/deepseek-v3-a-powerful-mixture-of-experts-model-64d8a86ac5ed?sharedUserId=tahirbalarabe2) by now. You only activate part of the model for each token. Saves compute. Good for scaling.

[🤖DeepSeek V3: A Powerful Mixture-of-Experts Model](https://medium.com/@tahirbalarabe2/deepseek-v3-a-powerful-mixture-of-experts-model-64d8a86ac5ed?sharedUserId=tahirbalarabe2)

Kimi K3 has 896 experts. Activates 16 per token. That’s 1.8% of the model at any time.

Compare that to other models:

- Nemotron 3 Ultra activates 4.3%
- Mixtral 8x7B activates 3.1%
- Deepseek V3 activates around 3.1%

Kimi K3 is the biggest open model and has the smallest activation ratio.

Here’s what that means in practice. When you spread this model across GPUs in a data center, every token has to travel between GPUs to activate its experts. With 896 experts spread across 64 or 72 GPUs, that’s a lot of travel. A lot of communication overhead.

Most people don’t think about this. They think about FLOPs. They think about parameter counts. But the real cost is moving data between chips. That’s where the bottleneck is.

Kimi K3 solves this with two innovations.

## Stable Latent MoE

First, they compress the token representation before routing it to experts. Instead of sending the full embedding across GPUs, they send a compressed version. Less data to move. Less overhead.

The process works like this:

- The token embedding goes through a down projection
- The compressed token routes to experts
- Two shared experts activate by default
- The rest flows through the pool of 896 experts
- Only 16 experts activate per token
- The result projects back up to the original dimension

The router chooses experts based on this compressed representation. Then after the experts do their work, the result gets projected back up to the original dimension.

This is stable latent MoE. The “stable” part refers to how they keep the router balanced during training. With 896 experts and only 16 selected, the router needs to be careful. Small variations can throw it off.

## Quantile Balancing

Instead of using bias terms to nudge the router toward underused experts, they use quantile balancing.

Think of it like grading on a curve rather than using raw scores. Each expert gets evaluated relative to the others. This helps maintain balance without the router getting confused.

The alternatives don’t work as well at this scale:

- [Deepseek V3 uses bias terms to penalize overused experts](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.19437)
- [Nemotron 3 Ultra](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/nemotron/Nemotron-3-Ultra/) uses similar bias nudging
- Kimi K3’s quantile approach works better with 896 experts

## Kimi Delta Attention

The second innovation is more important. It’s about attention.

Attention is expensive. Everyone knows this. But we also want huge context windows. A million tokens. The standard attention mechanism has quadratic complexity. As context grows, compute grows with the square of the context length. This gets painful fast.

There are many ways to fix this:

- Linear attention
- State space models (Mamba, Mamba 2)
- Recurrent neural networks
- Hybrid approaches

Kimi K3 uses something called Kimi Delta Attention. This builds on earlier work from October 2025.

## From RNNs to Delta Attention

The idea starts with recurrent neural networks. Information comes in. Memory updates. Old information decays.

But RNNs have a training problem. Sequential processing makes parallelization difficult. Transformers won because you could train them in parallel.

The researchers figured out how to get the best of both worlds. You get the memory efficiency of recurrence with the parallel training of transformers.

## Gated DeltaNet

Before Kimi Delta Attention, there was Gated DeltaNet. The idea was to improve how memory updates work:

```
New memory = decay * old memory + (new information - predicted)
```

Instead of overwriting memory with new values, you find the error. You only apply the error correction. More efficient.

But Gated DeltaNet had a problem. It couldn’t independently control how much information was retained versus forgotten. Everything decayed at the same rate.

## What Kimi Delta Changes

Kimi Delta Attention gives you fine-grained control channel by channel. Each channel can decay at a different rate.

This matters for hardware efficiency. The original Gated DeltaNet paper explicitly noted that implementing it efficiently on hardware was a challenge. Kimi K3 solves this with chunkwise parallelization.

They group multiple steps together to parallelize training and inference. The paper includes a hardware-efficient chunkwise algorithm that makes this work in practice.

## The Hybrid Approach

Kimi K3 interleaves Kimi Delta Attention with global attention at a 3:1 ratio:

- 3 Kimi Delta Attention layers
- 1 global attention layer (Gated MLA)

This creates a hybrid linear attention model by definition. The ablation study showed this was the ideal ratio.

## Attention Residual

There’s one more piece. Attention residual connections.

Residual connections are boring. Everyone uses them. But they matter more than people think.

## Why Residuals Matter

When you stack many layers, information gets distorted by the time it reaches the end. Residual connections create a path for the original input to flow through. Modifications get applied on top.

Think of it like plumbing. Without relief valves, pressure builds up. With proper plumbing, water flows freely.

Standard residual connections help. But they have a problem. Deep layers know very little about earlier layers. The signal gets diluted.

## What Attention Residual Changes

Attention residual changes this. Each layer can selectively pull information from earlier residual states.

For Kimi K3, they incorporate these into logical blocks. Group previous layers into bigger blocks. This prevents the connections from becoming too expensive.

The benefits are clear:

- Information flows between layers more effectively
- The model becomes more expressive
- Interconnect burden on GPUs decreases
- Training becomes more stable

The research paper for attention residual showed strong results. Models with attention residual performed better than those without. Kimi K3 adopted this approach and scaled it up.

## The Economics Question

Now we get to the part that matters.

Kimi K3 pricing is:

- $3 per million input tokens
- $15 per million output tokens

That’s already cheaper than many US models with comparable performance.

But here’s the thing. US hardware is better. NVIDIA H100s. B200s. Advanced networking. More memory bandwidth.

If you take this efficient architecture and run it on better hardware, what happens?

## The China Advantage

Chinese AI labs face real constraints:

- Limited access to advanced chips
- Export controls restricting supply
- Less overall compute capacity

These constraints forced them to think about efficiency. They had no choice.

Necessity is the mother of invention. When you can’t throw more hardware at a problem, you have to get smarter. You have to optimize.

## The US Dilemma

The US has the opposite problem. We have abundant compute. We got comfortable. We optimized less.

Now efficient Chinese models exist. They run on inferior hardware and still compete. Put them on superior US hardware and they might dominate.

The US government is talking about banning Kimi K3. That would be a mistake.

Think about it:

- The model is open
- The architecture is published
- The innovations are in the paper

If we ban the model, we don’t learn from it. We don’t see what works and what doesn’t. We don’t get to run it on our superior hardware and see how fast it can go.

## What Changes

The real competition isn’t about whether Chinese models are better. It’s about who can learn fastest from the innovations that appear.

The AI industry moves too fast for protectionism to work. By the time you ban one model, the next one is already out.

I’m curious to see how Kim K3 performs on US clouds. With better hardware, inference could be:

- Cheaper than $3 per million input tokens
- Faster than current throughput
- More capable with larger context windows

That’s the thing about efficiency. It doesn’t just benefit the people who invent it. It benefits anyone who can run the code. And if the code is open, everyone can run it.

## The Architecture Lesson

Kimi K3 shows us something important. Model architecture matters more than raw scale.

The innovations here are not small optimizations. They’re fundamental changes to how these models work:

- Stable latent MoE changes routing
- Kimi Delta Attention changes memory
- Attention residual changes information flow

Each one addresses a real bottleneck. Communication. Memory. Depth.

Together they create a model that’s more efficient than anything at this scale.

## The Innovation Lesson

The timeline matters here:

- Kimi Delta Attention: October 2025
- Attention Residual: March 2025
- Kimi K3 Release: January 2026

The model incorporates research that existed for months. It scales these ideas up. It makes them work in practice.

This is how progress happens. Not with a single breakthrough. With the combination of many small innovations.

## The Competitive Lesson

The question isn’t whether Chinese models will lead. The question is whether we’re smart enough to learn from them.

Every industry has moments like this. A competitor does something unexpected. The incumbents have a choice. They can dismiss it. They can ban it. Or they can study it and improve.

The companies that choose to study and improve usually win.

But that’s not a bad thing. Competition breeds innovation. Chinese efficiency forced everyone to think differently about what matters.

Efficiency has a compounding effect. A model that’s 2x more efficient is not just half the cost. It can be run on different hardware. In different configurations. For different use cases. It changes what’s possible.

That’s what I see in Kimi K3. A model that changes what’s possible. Not because it’s Chinese. Because it’s smart.

And the smartest thing we can do is pay attention.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

### 1\. What is the DeepSeek moment mentioned in the article?

The DeepSeek moment refers to January 2025, when DeepSeek caught up to OpenAI on benchmarks while training their model at a fraction of the GPU rental cost, signaling a shift in AI cost-efficiency.

### 2\. How does Kim K3 compare to DeepSeek and OpenAI models?

Kim K3 has nearly caught up to OpenAI and Anthropic in benchmarks, but more importantly, it introduces major architectural innovations like stable latent MoE and Kimi Delta Attention that improve efficiency and inference speed.

### 3\. What is Mixture of Experts (MoE) and why does it matter?

MoE activates only a small portion of a model’s parameters per token instead of the entire model. This reduces compute overhead. Kim K3 activates only 1.8% of its experts (16 out of 896), making it highly efficient.

### 4\. What makes Kim K3’s MoE different from previous models?

Kim K3 uses stable latent MoE, which compresses token embeddings into a lower dimension before routing to experts. This reduces communication overhead when models are spread across multiple GPUs in data centers.

### 5\. What is Kimi Delta Attention (KDA)?

KDA is a linear attention mechanism that replaces traditional quadratic attention. It provides fine-grained control over how memory is decayed channel-by-channel, improving both speed and accuracy during inference.

### 6\. How does Kim K3 handle long context windows efficiently?

By using a hybrid of 3 Kimi Delta Attention layers and 1 global attention layer (gated MLA), Kim K3 maintains a 1 million token context window without the massive compute cost of full attention.

### 7\. What is the activation ratio of Kim K3 and why is it important?

Kim K3 has a 1.8% activation ratio, meaning only 1.8% of its parameters are used per token. This is lower than competitors like Neimotron 3 Ultra (4.3%), making Kim K3 one of the most efficient large models available.

### 8\. How does Kim K3 optimize for multi-GPU deployment?

It uses stable latent MoE to compress token data, reducing the amount of information passed between GPUs. It also uses attention residual grouping to minimize interconnect burden, making it well-suited for Nvidia NVL72 racks and super nodes.

### 9\. What is attention residual and why is it useful?

Attention residual allows each layer to selectively pull information from earlier layers, preventing information dilution in deep networks. This improves model expressiveness and training stability.

### 10\. What are the current inference costs for Kim K3?

Kim K3 is priced at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens. The article suggests that US data centers with advanced chips could potentially run it even cheaper or faster.
